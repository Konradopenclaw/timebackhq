A fictional remodeling company. A real look at the method.

At a glance

This sample shows how TimeBackHQ turns a recurring handoff failure into a visible lead pipeline, a weekly owner brief, and clear approval boundaries without automating the owner's judgment.

Lead volume is not the problem; the handoff is.

Cedarline is a fictional 12-person residential remodeling company. Leads arrive through referrals, phone, email, and a web form. There is plenty of work. What is missing is one trusted record that carries a lead from first contact through an accepted project without the owner rebuilding the context at every step.

Interpretation drag

The owner must decide what incomplete notes mean before anyone can move.

Decision drag

Routine next steps wait because the decision rules are not visible.

Handoff drag

Information is rewritten when responsibility moves from office to owner, estimator, or project lead.

Follow-up drag

Customer updates and internal reminders depend on memory instead of a trusted queue.

What the record often looks like now

  • Stage: New, or blank
  • Lead source: missing
  • Owner: unassigned, or a name that no longer handles intake
  • Next action: empty
  • Next action due: empty
  • Last customer contact: unknown
  • Quote sent date and follow-up status: not recorded

A lead can be marked contacted with no next action scheduled. Nobody can tell the difference between a live opportunity and a dead one without asking the owner.

The primary constraint is context reconstruction at every handoff.

Cedarline does not need another place to store information. It needs a smaller number of required facts, a visible decision owner, and one next action that survives the handoff.

  1. Inquiry capture: information varies by channel, so the owner reconstructs basic facts.
  2. Qualification: fit rules live in the owner's head, so ordinary leads become custom decisions.
  3. Site visit and estimate: notes, photos, assumptions, and questions are scattered.
  4. Estimate follow-up: there is no single owner, next action, due date, and status.
  5. Project start: the accepted estimate does not carry all delivery context and open decisions.
  6. Changes and customer updates: a fluent draft can outrun approved scope, price, or schedule.

Seven priorities, in order.

  1. One lead record for every inquiry, every channel.
  2. Required fields before a record can move: source, stage, owner, next action, and due date.
  3. Quote-sent date, age, follow-up status, and next follow-up date.
  4. Weekly owner brief for overdue actions and exceptions.
  5. One Project Start Handoff Packet.
  6. Separate low-consequence drafting from customer commitments.
  7. One closeout lesson that improves the next workflow.

The assessment expands the highest-leverage items into concrete first-week actions like the two Quick Starts below.

Quick start 1: One lead pipeline and response lane.

Objective

Give the office coordinator and owner one reliable view of every new inquiry and its next action.

Tangible first-week target

By the end of the first working week, Cedarline can open one queue, see what happens next, and route routine inquiries without asking the owner to reconstruct the record.

Approval boundary

The office coordinator can use the written fit rules for ordinary cases. The owner decides exceptions. No automation accepts a project, quotes a price, or commits to a visit date.

Weekly owner brief

The owner sees new leads by source, records with no owner or next action, overdue actions, quotes grouped by age, items waiting on customer information, and exceptions that require a decision.

Example minimum lead record fields and sample reply
  • Contact
  • Project type
  • General service-area location
  • Timing
  • Budget posture, if known
  • Lead source: phone, web form, referral, text, or email
  • Known constraints
  • Missing information
  • Stage: New, Contact Attempted, Qualified, Site Visit Needed, Quote in Progress, Quote Sent, Won, Lost, or Not a Fit
  • Owner
  • Next action
  • Due date
  • Last meaningful customer contact
  • Quote sent date and quote age, when applicable
  • Follow-up status: not due, due today, overdue, paused, or owner review
  • Exact exception decision, if one is needed

Sample “more information needed” reply

Hi Example Homeowner,

Thank you for reaching out about your kitchen refresh. Before we schedule the next conversation, could you reply with the city or general service-area location, the parts of the kitchen you expect to change, your preferred timing, whether the layout or plumbing locations need to move, and any access or occupied-home constraints we should understand?

Please do not send financial records, identification documents, alarm codes, or other sensitive information. Once we have the basic project context, we will review it and respond with the appropriate next step.

Thank you,
Cedarline Remodel Works

These stages, fields, and thresholds are illustrative. A real client's pipeline would be tailored to its own workflow and existing tools.

Quick start 2: One Project Start Handoff Packet.

Objective

Give the project lead a complete operational handoff when an estimate becomes accepted work.

Packet contents

  • Approved scope and explicit exclusions
  • Customer priorities and access constraints
  • Open decisions with owners and due dates
  • Communication preference
  • Source links to the approved estimate and supporting files
  • The first five internal actions

Filled fictional scope example

Remove existing cabinet fronts, counters, sink, and selected lighting; install customer-approved replacements and repaint affected surfaces. Layout and plumbing stay unchanged. Appliances are customer-supplied and must be onsite by the agreed installation date. Structural work, electrical-panel changes, and repairs outside the documented kitchen work area remain excluded unless approved through a separate written change.

The packet records approval; it does not create it.

May do

  • Request missing information
  • Draft status updates
  • Schedule internal preparation
  • Flag a possible change

May not do without authorized human approval

  • Change price or scope
  • Promise a date outside the confirmed schedule
  • Approve a change order
  • Waive a contract condition
  • Send an AI-generated customer commitment as approved fact

Do not automate customer quotes or change-order approval.

That decision combines scope, price, schedule, contract terms, and customer context. A fluent draft can hide an unsupported assumption, and a wrong answer can create a real financial or relationship commitment.

Better control

  1. Use a standard change-request intake.
  2. Record the requested change and its operational effect.
  3. Route the decision to the authorized person.
  4. Generate a draft only after the decision fields are complete.
  5. Require human approval before any customer-facing send.

Simplify the decision path first. Automate only the low-consequence drafting around an approved decision.

Tangible proof in week one. Greater value over the next two weeks.

Week 1

Put the lead record, response lanes, visible action ownership, and exception rules into use inside an existing tool.

Week 2

Extend the same discipline into the Project Start Handoff Packet.

Week 3

Review repetitive reformatting, test only draft-first support with fictional data, name the approval and fallback, and reject unclear automation.

This is Cedarline's recommended operating sequence. It does not add three weeks of TimeBackHQ implementation to the $999 assessment.
